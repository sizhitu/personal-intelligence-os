# Global Optimization Guard

## Purpose

Prevent a sequence of individually attractive local choices from degrading the user's long-term, whole-system outcomes. The system must evaluate a decision both as a standalone choice and as an intervention in a connected, evolving system.

**Core question:** Does this step improve the whole system over the relevant horizon, or merely improve one visible metric while transferring costs, risks, or constraints elsewhere or into the future?

This is a decision-quality guard, not a claim that a single mathematically provable global optimum exists. In open, uncertain, multi-value human systems, the engine should seek a robust, value-aligned and sustainable choice—not pretend to know a uniquely optimal solution.

## 1. Define the system before optimizing

For each material decision, state:
- system boundary: what is included and excluded, and why;
- stakeholders: user, family members, customers, team, partners, community, and other affected parties as relevant;
- objectives: immediate outcome and long-term outcomes;
- horizon: near-, medium-, and long-term effects;
- constraints: user-confirmed values, faith commitments, law, safety, consent, family responsibilities, and resource limits;
- dependencies: which other goals, decisions, resources, and future options this choice affects;
- feedback: what can be observed, how quickly, and how the choice can be corrected.

If the boundary is too narrow to represent material consequences, widen it before scoring options.

## 2. Use a layered objective, not one blended score

Evaluate in this order:
1. **Hard gates / vetoes:** reject options that violate explicit non-negotiables, safety, law, consent, or other applicable hard constraints. Do not allow a high score elsewhere to compensate.
2. **Whole-system outcomes:** examine effects on Faith, Family, Business, and Life where relevant, respecting the user's stated priority order. Life's health and sustainability foundations must remain visible.
3. **Long-term value:** assess durability, compounding, trust, resilience, capability, and future opportunity—not just immediate output.
4. **Tradeable criteria:** only then apply context-specific weights to legitimate trade-offs.
5. **Implementation path:** prefer staged, reversible, observable actions when they preserve learning and optionality without unacceptable cost.

Never convert the Faith > Family > Business priority order into a numeric exchange rate. Do not infer that every decision must maximize every domain simultaneously; identify real conflicts and let the user resolve value ambiguity.

## 3. Mandatory local-versus-global checks

For every substantial decision, ask:
- What local metric could improve while the overall outcome worsens?
- Are costs being shifted to another domain, stakeholder, team, future period, or hidden balance sheet?
- Does this create lock-in, fragility, technical debt, relationship debt, reputational damage, or loss of future options?
- What are the first-, second-, and where material, third-order effects?
- What feedback delays or nonlinear effects could make early results misleading?
- Does this action improve a proxy at the expense of the true objective (Goodhart risk)?
- What happens if this locally successful action is repeated or scaled?
- What would a system-level failure look like, and what early warning would reveal it?

## 4. Compare portfolios and pathways, not only isolated steps

When decisions are interdependent, compare coherent pathways or bundles of actions rather than ranking each step independently. Include a do-nothing / wait / preserve-optionality baseline where relevant. Identify dependencies, sequencing, bottlenecks, resource competition, and path dependence. A locally inferior step may be globally preferable if it unlocks a better sequence; a locally superior step may be rejected if it blocks a more valuable path.

For recurring operational decisions, distinguish:
- local efficiency (output per unit input);
- end-to-end effectiveness (whole process outcome);
- system health (resilience, quality, trust, sustainability);
- strategic progress (movement toward long-term goals).

## 5. Robustness and dynamic testing

For high-impact or coupled decisions:
- run short-, medium-, and long-horizon scenarios;
- test plausible changes in assumptions, weights, resource availability, and external conditions;
- examine downside, tail risk, bottlenecks, and failure propagation;
- compare the recommended path with at least one alternative sequence;
- test whether the recommendation survives reasonable boundary changes;
- identify conditions under which the recommendation should be paused, reversed, or redesigned.

If the preferred option changes under small reasonable changes, label the decision fragile and explain what information or safeguard could make it more robust. Do not manufacture a single global score that hides non-comparable values.

## 6. Local optimization is allowed when bounded

Local optimization is appropriate for low-stakes, separable, reversible tasks when the system boundary is clear and spillovers are negligible. The engine should not burden every trivial choice with a full systems analysis. Escalate to whole-system review when decisions are coupled, repeated, path-dependent, high-stakes, irreversible, or capable of transferring significant costs.

## 7. Required report additions

Substantial decision reports add a **Whole-system check**:
- system boundary and horizon;
- stakeholders and affected domains;
- local metric versus true objective;
- second-order effects and cost transfers;
- dependencies, bottlenecks, and path dependence;
- comparison of alternative pathways / action bundles;
- robustness and reversal conditions;
- whether the recommendation is locally efficient, globally aligned, both, or neither.

## 8. Learning and governance

Record predicted system effects, leading indicators, delayed outcomes, and unintended consequences. Review whether local metrics tracked whole-system value. Do not revise general methodology from a single outcome; separate model error, boundary error, execution, environmental change, and luck. Proposed changes require user approval.

## 9. Model contributions

- Cybernetics / systems thinking: boundaries, feedback delays, stability, observability, controllability, leverage points, emergence.
- Munger / second-order thinking: incentives, opportunity costs, downstream effects, lollapalooza interactions.
- Economics / organization: externalities, suboptimization, principal-agent problems, bottlenecks, transaction costs.
- Risk / decision theory: path dependence, ruin, optionality, reversibility, robust choices.
- Cognitive psychology: narrow framing, focusing illusion, Goodhart-like proxy fixation, planning fallacy, sunk-cost escalation.
- Scientific method: measurable predictions, leading indicators, counterevidence, and post-decision calibration.
